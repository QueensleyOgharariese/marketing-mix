Sooner or later it happens to everyone: a faulty product, a delay, a mistake in an ad. You can't always avoid a crisis, but you can stop it growing because of how you respond.

## The 6 steps

### 1. Get the facts

Before speaking: what happened, to how many people, why, what can be done. Ask the people who know (warehouse, customer service, quality). Speaking before you know leads to wrong promises.

### 2. Stop what makes it worse

Cheerful scheduled posts, promotions, newsletters: check what's going out in the next few hours and pause anything that would seem insensitive.

### 3. The public message

Short and honest:

- **what happened**, without downplaying it;
- **an apology**;
- **the remedy** for those affected;
- **how to contact you**;
- if needed, **what you're changing** so it doesn't happen again.

### 4. Reply to everyone

Same core message, personal words. Don't hide criticism. How to handle comments is explained in [community management](/en/blog/community-management/).

### 5. Talk to journalists

- Reply **by the deadline** they give.
- Use **only verified facts**.
- Name a **spokesperson** and a contact.
- No "no comment", no speculation, no blaming others.
- Remember that **nothing is truly "off the record"**.

### 6. The summary

Once it's over: the facts, what was done, what worked and what to change.

## An example

At the fictional tea shop Cinque Foglie, about 40 matcha kits arrived with a broken whisk. Comments piled up, a creator talked about it in her stories, and a journalist from a tea magazine asked whether all the kits were faulty.

The response: check with the warehouse (40 kits, damaged in transit, the rest fine), pause that day's promotion, a message with an apology and a free new whisk for everyone affected, a personal reply to every comment, a message to the creator, and a reply to the journalist with the facts, the remedy, the new rigid case and the spokesperson's contact. The article that came out called it a "quick, transparent response".

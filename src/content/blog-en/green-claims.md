"Green", "eco", "sustainable", "zero impact": for years these words appeared on all sorts of products. Often without proof. It's called **greenwashing**, and in the European Union the rules have got stricter.

## What changed

**Directive (EU) 2024/825**, which applies from **27 September 2026**, added to the list of unfair commercial practices, among other things:

- **generic environmental claims** not backed by recognised excellent environmental performance ("sustainable", "eco-friendly", "green", "environmentally friendly");
- claims that a product has a **neutral, reduced or positive** climate impact based on **offsetting** emissions;
- **sustainability labels** not based on a certification scheme or set up by public authorities;
- claims about the whole product when they only concern one part.

## What to write instead

Precise, verifiable facts:

| Instead of… | Write… |
| --- | --- |
| Sustainable packaging | Aluminium tin, recyclable with metal waste |
| Eco pack | Inner bag compostable, certified EN 13432 |
| Zero-impact shipping | We offset shipping emissions through a reforestation project |
| Green box | Box made from 80% recycled cardboard |

A good claim says **which part**, **what** is different about it and **with what proof** (certification, percentage). It often adds **how to dispose of it**.

## An example

The back of the fictional tea shop Cinque Foglie's box said: *"100% sustainable packaging. Zero-impact shipping."* A customer wrote: *"You say sustainable but there was bubble wrap in the parcel."* She was right: since October the filler had been plastic.

The new line uses only facts from the quality team's sheet: recyclable tin, certified compostable bag, 80% recycled cardboard. No "100%", no "zero impact".

Sustainability words only build a brand if they're true: a line disproved by a customer costs more than a line never written.

Writing articles is easy. Writing the **right** articles, the ones people search for and that help the site grow, takes a plan. That plan is the **content strategy**.

## What is it for?

A content strategy answers four questions:

1. **Who** are we writing for?
2. **What** do we talk about (and what not)?
3. **Why**: what goal do we want to reach?
4. **How** do we know it's working?

Without these answers you end up publishing unconnected articles that bring neither visits nor customers.

## The 5 steps

### 1. The audience

Describe your readers: what they already know, what they're missing, what problems they have. For the fictional Cinque Foglie shop: people who drink tea every day but aren't experts and want to understand what they're buying.

### 2. The topics (pillars)

Choose 3-5 big topics and stick to them. For Cinque Foglie: making tea, choosing tea, herbal teas for different times of day, origins and producers.

### 3. The keywords

For each topic, find the questions people ask on Google, with volume and intent. How to do it is explained in [how to choose keywords](/en/blog/how-to-choose-keywords/). Organise them in groups: a **main article** (pillar) on a broad topic and a few **supporting articles** (cluster) on specific questions, linked to each other.

### 4. The calendar

Decide the pace and the order: the most useful articles first, plus seasonal ones (hot tea guides before autumn, gift ideas before November).

### 5. Measurement

For each article, choose what to watch: impressions and clicks from Google, reading time, clicks to products. Check after a few months, because SEO isn't instant.

## An example

| Topic | Main article | Supporting articles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Making tea | How to make green tea | Water temperature; How long to steep; Why green tea tastes bitter |
| Choosing tea | Types of tea: the guide | Green or black tea?; What is Sencha; Matcha for beginners |

Each supporting article links to the main one and vice versa: for Google it's a sign that the site knows the topic well.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about what interests the company, not the readers.
- Publishing a lot at the start and then disappearing.
- Never updating old articles: updating one often pays off more than writing a new one.
- Making health claims in articles about tea and herbal teas: in the European Union, only authorised claims can be used for food.

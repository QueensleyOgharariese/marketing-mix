20|What SEO is and how Google works
21|Organic results and ads
22|When you search on Google you see two kinds of results. Ads, marked «Sponsored», are paid for every click: that's the SEA job. Organic results are free: Google ranks them on its own, based on how well each page answers the search. SEO (Search Engine Optimization) is the work of getting a site's pages to appear high in the organic results. Today, for some searches, Google also shows an AI-written summary (AI Overviews) that cites some pages as sources.

29|How Google finds and ranks pages
30|Google works in three phases. Crawling: a program (Googlebot) follows links and reads pages. Indexing: Google saves the pages it finds useful in its archive, the index. Ranking: when someone searches, Google picks the most useful pages from the index and puts them in order. A page that isn't crawled or doesn't make it into the index can't appear, however beautiful it is.

37|The three parts of the job
38|SEO has three parts. Content: choosing the right searches and writing pages that answer them well, with clear titles, descriptions and copy. Technical: making sure Google can read the site without obstacles, meaning fast pages, no errors, tidy URLs. Authority: other trusted websites talking about us and linking to our site (backlinks).

48|How it's measured
49|SEO work is measured with four numbers, read in Google Search Console. Impressions: how many times a page appears in the results. Clicks: how many times someone clicks it. CTR: clicks divided by impressions, as a percentage. Average position: where it appears in the ranking. Then, with Google Analytics, you see what happens after the click: visits, orders, revenue.
50|The green teas page has 10,000 impressions and 100 clicks: the CTR is 100 / 10,000 = 1%. Out of 100 people who see it, only one clicks.

77|Keywords, intent and content
78|Keywords and volume
79|A keyword is what a person types into Google. Volume is how many times a month it's searched. A high-volume keyword can bring the most visits, but usually has more competition; a longer, more specific keyword has less volume but more decided people. Each page gets one main keyword and a few close ones.

87|Search intent
88|Behind every search there's an intent, meaning what the person really wants. Informational: they want to understand («how to brew sencha»). Commercial: they compare before choosing («best green tea»). Transactional: they want to buy («organic sencha price»). Navigational: they're looking for a specific site («cinque foglie»). Google shows pages that match the intent: guides for an informational search, product pages for a transactional one.
89|For «how to brew sencha» a product page never ranks: Google prefers a guide. For «organic sencha price» it's the other way round.

101|One page per intent
102|Each page should answer one specific intent. If two pages on the same site target the same search, they compete: Google alternates between them and neither ranks. It's called keyword cannibalisation. You fix it by giving each page a different job and linking them together.
103|The guide «Sencha: what it is and how to brew it» explains; the product page «Organic Sencha green tea» sells. The guide links to the product page for people who want to buy.
104|Two pages targeting the same search help the site appear more often.
105|They split the clicks and Google doesn't know which to choose: usually neither ranks.
106|Title, meta description and H1
107|The title is the blue title in Google's results: it should contain the keyword, say what people will find when they click and stay within about 580 pixels (60 characters). The meta description is the grey text below: 100-160 characters that persuade people to click. It doesn't improve rankings, but it increases clicks. The H1 is the big heading on the page, read by visitors.

120|Useful, trustworthy content
121|Google rewards content written for people: useful, clear, written by someone who knows the topic. Its guidelines use the acronym E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness, trustworthiness. For a food shop there are also legal rules: in the European Union you can't promise unauthorised health effects (Regulation EC 1924/2006), such as «burns fat» or «detox».
122|«Our Sencha is brewed at 70-80 °C, for 1-2 minutes» is useful and true. «The Sencha that makes you lose weight» is banned and loses trust.

150|Technical SEO, tools and numbers
151|Getting a page into (or out of) Google
152|The «noindex» meta robots tag asks Google not to show a page: it's used for the cart, account area and service pages. The robots.txt file, instead, tells Google which parts of the site not to crawl. Careful: if a page is blocked in robots.txt, Google can't read its noindex; to remove a page from the results you use noindex, not robots.txt.
153|Cinque Foglie's cart has a noindex: that's fine. If the noindex ended up on a category by mistake, that category would disappear from Google.

163|Redirects and 404 pages
164|When a page changes URL or is removed, you use a redirect. A 301 is permanent: Google passes the old page's value to the new one. A 302 is temporary. If a URL no longer exists and there's no redirect, anyone opening it gets a 404 error. A redirect should always point to the most similar page, and without chains (a redirect leading to another redirect).

172|Duplicate content and canonical
173|The same content can have several URLs: for example a category sorted by price (/te-verdi/?ordina=prezzo). For Google they're copies, and it has to guess which one to show. The canonical is a hint in the code saying which version is the main one. That way filters stay available for shoppers and Google shows the right page.
174|/te-verdi/?ordina=prezzo points its canonical to /te-verdi/: only the category appears on Google.

176|The main version of a page that has copies
177|That the page must not appear on Google
178|Where to redirect people opening the page
179|The canonical doesn't hide or move anything: it says which version to treat as the real one.
180|Speed: Core Web Vitals
181|Google measures the experience of real visits with Core Web Vitals. LCP: how long the largest element takes to appear (good under 2.5 seconds). INP: how quickly the page responds to a click (good under 200 milliseconds). CLS: how much the page shifts while loading (good under 0.1). Phones and computers are looked at separately.
